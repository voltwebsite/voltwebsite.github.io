  • Scientific Annual Report 2025

    Annual highlights

    The Center for Volatile Interactions (VOLT) aims to provide fundamental insights into the production, consumption, and transformations of volatile trace gasses in ecosystems. A major highlight of 2025 was the successful 1st International Symposium of Volatile Interactions in Ecosystems, a conference planned and organized by VOLT. The VOLT symposium gathered close to 100 scientists from across the world to present and discuss advances within our scientific discipline (Fig. 1). The success of the symposium led to an agreement to continue with a second symposium within a few years. The symposium was preceded by a PhD course “Biogenic Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs) and Isotope Techniques”. The course was fully booked and both the ~30 PhD students and the international experts that joined the VOLT team in teaching were pleased with the intense course days. The PhD course was organized by a team of VOLT post docs, which gave them valuable management experience.

    2025 was a year of funding success for VOLT. Yi Jiao, a VOLT assistant professor, became a new group leader as he obtained a Villum Young Investigator grant with the focus on peatland biogeochemistry. The center received no less than four Marie Curie post doc fellowships and a post doc fellowship from the Swedish Research Council as well as Villum Experiment and Carlsberg Infrastructure grants. The VOLT center director, Riikka Rinnan, was selected as a Lead Author for the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) 7th Assessment Report.

    In terms of research activities, the third center year was filled with VOLT’s spirit: aiming for collaboration instead of competition. Led by VOLT post doc Nasmille Larke-Mejía, we developed a key method, an innovative high throughput assay for separating production and consumption processes for isoprene, an important VOC with global emission rates similar to methane. This assay was used in several collaborative projects involving students and staff. Overall, post doc PhD collaborations flourished, some originally planned, others established by own initiative of the early-career researchers.

    In 2025, we want to highlight four publications illustrating the diversity of our research:

    “Net emission of atmospheric volatile organic compounds from ponds in a peatland forest”, a research article published in Limnology and Oceanography.

    “Increased growth temperatures alter Arctic plant responses to heat wave and drought”, a research article published in Global Change Biology.

    “Extreme smells—microbial production of volatile organic compounds at the limits of life”, a review article published in FEMS Microbiology Reviews.

    “Time to end the vascular plant chauvinism”, a correspondence published in Nature Plants.

    The Scientific Advisory Board (SAB) for VOLT comprises Jaana Bäck, Jörg Peter Schnitzler, Hans-Peter Grossart, and Colin Murrell, all leading experts with complementary backgrounds relevant to VOLT. In 2025, the SAB remotely attended parts of the program for the Annual Visit by the DNRF. This was followed by discussions about progress and future strategy between SAB and the VOLT PIs.

    The post doc 5 (modelling of microbial processes) position was filled as Jonathan Donhauser started in August 2025. Previous post doc 2, Cecilia Costas Selas, shifted to her own Marie Curie funding in July 2025, still at VOLT. Sana Mariya James was hired as research assistant to assist in various laboratory analysis, data processing, and writing tasks.     Recruitment and gender strategy

    VOLT aims to attract the best candidates internationally by clearly communicating the unique research and training offered in the center. Recruitment is done using public advertisement of positions both via official channels, personal contacts, and social media. Usually hiring interviews are held first online and then on site and the selection is based on excellence.

    In 2025, VOLT center director, Riikka Rinnan, was contacted by many high-profile scientists within the research field enquiring about possibilities to join VOLT. One of these led to a Recruit Sabbatical application to Novo Nordisk Foundation, which was granted and allowed Dr. Kolby Jardine from Berkeley National Laboratory to join the center in 2026. In addition, several other collaborative funding proposals were submitted to allow top international scientists to join VOLT.

    VOLT endorses diversity, as demonstrated by the gender-balanced and multinational team of PIs and collaborators. The PI team comprises three females and two males, and collaborators are roughly 50% of each gender. Among post doc, PhD student and TAP staff, 54% are female and 46% male. VOLT members represent 19 different nationalities.

    The inclusive, open, and collaborative research environment is built to allow different approaches and personalities to contribute to a unique, creative space for science, which will translate into innovation and high productivity.

    Research integrity and data management

    VOLT is committed to the Danish Code of Conduct for Research Integrity and to following FAIR principles for scientific management of data. In January 2025, we launched a database in the Electronic Research Data Archive at the University of Copenhagen (ERDA). The architecture, instructions, and templates for meta-data and datasets were built by post doc/data scientist Tamás Plaszkó, after discussions with VOLT members and with a dedicated working group consisting of Dr. Plaszkó, Jing Tang, and Anders Priemé. The database aims to provide a long-term data sharing platform for VOLT, take into account the complexity, multidimensionality and sheer size of our research data.

    In addition to our database, the raw data generated are backed up on several network drives to avoid accidental data loss. The data safety framework of ERDA has further strengthened our efforts to secure our data now and in the future. Shared working folders on ERDA are used for collaborative work, internally and externally. We maintain data analysis scripts and workflows on GitHub, using version control to ensure reproducible results, clear documentation of code changes, and efficient collaboration across the team.

    When starting at VOLT, all employees and students are introduced to and sign a document committing to responsible conduct of research, which is stored by the Center Administrator. All PhD students and postdocs also attend a mandatory course on responsible research conduct offered by the University of Copenhagen. Onboarding materials, protocols, infrastructure booking schedules, etc. are shared electronically via the VOLT Wiki page, also hosted on ERDA. VOLT has silver accreditation in the Laboratory Efficiency Assessment Framework (LEAF) aiming to provide sustainable and efficient lab practices.

    Research plan

    In 2025, several laboratory- and field-based experiments were conducted, data processing from previous years continued, and several manuscripts were submitted. Collectively, research activities have progressed according to the original plans and no significant changes have been made. The strong influx of post doc fellows with own funding enabled us to allocate more effort to selected key VOLT research topics. The efforts thus far hold great promise for advancing the understanding of drivers and dynamics of VOC emissions from various ecosystems. As our research tracks (RT) span across the different ecosystem/organism types and many studies contain elements from several RTs, we present the activities here organized according to the ecosystem/organism types.

    Freshwater ecosystems

    PhD student Ellen Slater and co-authors submitted their manuscript on VOCs from aquatic plants (macrophytes) to the journal Aquatic Botany (RT1). This work, which is the result of collaboration with French, British and Canadian researchers, characterizes the production of VOCs from four common species of submerged macrophytes in Lake Geneva, Switzerland. Ellen Slater has been on parental leave since spring 2025 and returns in April 2026.

    Research on water- atmosphere exchange of VOCs and greenhouse gases in Arctic freshwater systems was also carried out. Here, PhD student Ida Roos Friis conducted a one-month-long field campaign in the High Arctic of Canada, while two MSc students collected data from thermokarst ponds in Subarctic Sweden. In both locations, we measured CO2, CH4, and VOC fluxes in different Arctic lakes. These studies aim to reveal differences and similarities in VOCs emitted from distinct lakes and hereby identify the ecological drivers of VOC emission rates and compositions (RT1, 2).

    Marine ecosystems

    Postdoc Mehrshad Foroughan prepared, tested, and validated an improved setup for air-sea exchange measurements of VOCs at the Finnish Utö Atmospheric and Marine Research Station in the Baltic Sea. Building on the 2024 campaign, the setup featured key improvements including shorter inlet lines and an enhanced heating system. Most notably, measurements covered an extended period from April to October 2025, with logistics partially supported by an EU Aquarius grant awarded to Mehrshad in early 2025.

    In summer, PhD student Eve Galen went several times to Utö Island. She carried out VOC analyses with the aim to couple seawater VOC concentrations to phytoplankton community dynamics as documented by advanced imaging methodology, in collaboration with Finnish Environment Institute (RT1-3). Eve Galen and postdoc Cecilia Costas Selas went on a cruise to waters south-east of Greenland. The aim was to characterize VOCs emitted from these cold waters but also analyze the importance of the ongoing phytoplankton bloom. Data on VOC profiles, environmental parameters, and bacterial and phytoplankton community composition (16S rRNA gene sequences) are available, and the goal is to submit the work to a scientific journal in 2026 (RT1-3).

    Cecilia Costas Selas also headed a mesocosm experiment in the VOLT facilities in the Marine Biological Section in Helsingør. The experiment, with participation by Eve Galen and several undergraduate students, revealed that phytoplankton bloom formation is a main driver of marine VOC emission (RT1). Also, the forceful combination of VOC rate measurements and molecular characterization of the bacterioplankton and phytoplankton communities will pave the way for a good publication on this in 2026 (RT3).

    Finally, Cecilia Costas Selas and VOLT PI Lasse Riemann initiated a time-course sampling of Øresund with the aim to follow seasonal dynamics in VOC emissions from this area over a full year (RT1, 2).

    Soils

    The peatland biogeochemistry group, including assistant professor Yi Jiao, PhD student Mathilda Bloch Carlsson, visiting post doc Junyu Dong, and three MSc students conducted fieldwork in Storemosse National Park in southern Sweden in collaboration with a group from Radboud University, the Netherlands. They aimed to assess how climate change-related changes in water table depth and vegetation composition affect VOC dynamics in peat (RT1, 2).

    Post doc Kajsa Roslund conducted exploratory measurements of soil VOCs and greenhouse gases in a pristine Greenlandic ecosystem (Nûssuaq), with known natural geogenic gas and oil seeps (RT1). This project was done in collaboration with Aarhus University and Heidelberg University, Germany. Dr. Roslund has also led a collaborative effort with University of Lisbon, Portugal and Globe Institute, University of Copenhagen, to assess chemical composition of the air inside caves in Southern Portugal and linking the air volatile composition with the microbiome data from the caves. Around 200 microorganisms were isolated from the cave sediment and VOC production by these bacteria and fungi was also measured (RT1, 3). The sequencing results are completed and a manuscript summarizing the whole work are in preparation.

    VOLT team has also supervised student projects focusing on changes in VOC emission and consumption patterns during soil drying and after rewetting, and on the effects of temperature and soil type on VOC emissions (RT2). VOLT PhD student Neel Lindsby, who has two manuscripts in preparation on soil VOC emissions and consumption, was on parental leave most of 2025.

    Together with collaborators from the Department of Chemistry, University of Copenhagen,
    Anders Priemé was involved in developing a biotrickling filter using methane-consuming bacteria. The filter was able to bring down methane concentrations to unprecedented low concentrations and is likely to be employable at livestock production facilities and other methane emission hot spots. A manuscript will be submitted in early 2026.

    Plants and plant surface microbial communities

    Our research on plant volatiles and plant surface microbial communities included both field work and laboratory experiments. During her research stay abroad, PhD student Marta Contreras Serrano conducted field work in beetle-infested Australian forests. She assessed effects of the wood-boring beetle, Phoracantha mastersi, on snow gum (Eucalyptus pauciflora) VOC emissions, and observed a 62- fold increase in monoterpene emissions from the infested stems (RT3). This large response has both ecological and atmospheric consequences. The resulting manuscript is currently under review.

    In October 2025, a team of four VOLT researchers travelled to Yunnan (southwest China) to sample mosses and tree leaves across canopy-height gradients, along with soils beneath dominant tree species in a tropical rainforest, a rubber plantation, and savanna sites. The campaign aimed to quantify how moss nitrogen fixation, leaf functional traits, and VOC emissions vary within canopy and among dominant species across contrasting tropical ecosystems (RT1).

    Two large, collaborative, manuscripts were finalized and submitted documenting VOC emissions resulting from insect outbreaks in mountain birch forests (RT3). A manuscript led by post doc Jolanta Rieksta reports a three-year-long dataset demonstrating how the VOC emissions change up to and throughout an insect outbreak. A separate study, led by PI Jing Tang, combines field measurements, modelling and remote sensing approaches and shows that plant volatile emissions caused by insect outbreaks have effects cascading through aerosols and clouds on the regional weather (RT1-4). This manuscript is under consideration to one of the highest-ranking natural science journals after an extensive revision.

    Cryptogams

    Post doc Nasmille Laerke-Mejía and PhD student Annika Engroff have conducted experiments, using mosses as model organisms, assessing isoprene consumption mechanisms using a selective and a broad enzyme inhibitor to tease apart different processes and effects of temperature on the processes (RT1-3). The preliminary results suggest that isoprene production by mosses is higher than previously thought as a large fraction is consumed by isoprene-oxidizing bacteria.

    We began to identify how mosses control their nitrogen (N)- fixing associates (i.e., cyanobacteria). Postdoc Mingyue Yuan combined N addition experiments with gene expression of moss and cyanobacterial-specific genes to reveal that mosses use their immune system to repel N-fixing associates within 30 minutes upon N addition (RT3). When N is readily available, N-fixing associates are no longer beneficial but rather costly to the moss host and are expelled.

    In one of the first assessments of reactive N gas emissions (e.g. nitrous acid, HONO) from mosses, BSc student Clara Niemann (enrolled as MSc+PhD student at VOLT from February 2026) has shown that indeed, certain mosses can emit HONO under low moisture and high N availability (RT1). Given that mosses cover large fractions of the ground in e.g. forest and tundra ecosystems, emissions of the climate-relevant gas HONO can have large impacts on atmospheric chemistry.

    The cyanobacteria-moss team, led by VOLT PI Kathrin Rousk, embarked on an intensive field campaign in a Mediterranean forest on Sardinia, Italy. They collected biocrusts for laboratory experiments of HONO and VOC emissions (RT1), but most crucially, they conducted the first field measurements of N fixation in mosses in mediterranean climates with high time resolution. This data, combined with data from other climate zones, will be used to upscale N fixation in mosses globally. First analyses show that, interestingly, N fixation activity is high during night-time, and is strongly driven by moisture availability (RT2).

    Modelling

    Our modelling team was strengthened by three postdocs who focus on different scales. Postdoc Zhenqian Wang, with expertise in earth system modelling, began exploring biogenic VOC interactions with anthropogenic emissions in the Arctic region and finalized testing of the coupled biochemical feedback from VOCs in the Earth system model EC-Earth (RT4). Postdoc Wim Verbruggen, with a strong background in ecosystem modelling, quantitatively assesses heatwave impacts on VOC emissions and associated climatic feedback. Postdoc Jonathan Donhauser, a microbiologist with extensive experience handling genomic data and linking it to macro-scale responses, began working on a dynamic microbial model, DEMENTpy, with support from our key collaborator, Prof. Steven Allison (UC Irvine) (RT4).

    Postdoc Hongliang Ma completed the development of subdaily global canopy temperature products based on large datasets, which better capture plant thermal stress, especially under heatwave and drought conditions. PhD student Marta Contreras Serrano derived parameters from photosynthesis and VOC emission response curves and integrated these parameters into the dynamic ecosystem model, LPJ-GUESS, to reassess CO2 and VOC fluxes from Arctic shrub ecosystems. PhD student Ida Roos Friis evaluated ALBM (Advanced Lake Biogeochemical Model)-simulated lake CO2 and CH4 fluxes against measurement data and found limitations in the model’s ability to capture fluxes. Manuscripts on the modelling activities were prepared in 2025.

  • Scientific Annual Report 2024

    Annual Highlights

    The Center for Volatile Interactions (VOLT) aims to provide fundamental insights into the production, consumption, and transformations of volatile trace gasses in ecosystems. The year was active, productive and stimulating: VOLT hosted many guest researchers, established new collaborations, conducted several experiments and participated in successful field campaigns. All these activities reflect VOLT’s spirit: aiming for collaboration instead of competition.

    VOLT’s target organisms and systems include plant-soil continuums, cryptogams (mosses, lichens, and organic crusts), oceans, lakes, and the microbial communities living in these systems. In 2024, our work included elements from each of these, but had some emphasis on the aquatic systems. We kicked off the year with a mini-symposium on freshwater trace gas fluxes to discuss challenges and possibilities facing our work. The mini-symposium had about 20 participants, including international visitors from three countries, colleagues from the rest of the department as well as VOLT members.

    In 2024, VOLT developed new methods and optimized old ones for quantification of volatilomes (mixtures of different volatile organic compounds, VOCs) in water. The methods include immersive sampling of VOCs on probes, purging and trapping of volatiles out of water, operation of floating chambers to measure VOC emissions from ponds and lakes to the atmosphere as well as direct measurement of sea atmosphere exchange of VOCs using micrometeorological methods. These methodological advances facilitate efficient characterization of VOC emissions from aquatic ecosystems.

    In addition to the internal center events, such as the annual retreat and biweekly VOLT-all meetings and seminars, the center members were active in organizing international conferences. For example, VOLT PIs Lasse Riemann and Jing Tang chaired the Gordon Research Conference on Marine Microbes and the Gordon Research Seminar on Biogenic Hydrocarbons and the Atmosphere, respectively. The center received recognition in the form of funding: A new Marie Curie post doc fellow, Dr. Nasmille Laerke-Mejia, was welcomed to VOLT with a project aiming to quantify microbial degradation of isoprene, an important VOC, in Arctic ecosystems. Two center PIs, Kathrin Rousk and Jing Tang, received project grants from the Independent Research Fund Denmark. VOLT PI Anders Priemé was selected as the Teacher of the Year at the Department of Biology and nominated for Faculty’s teaching prize.

    invited perspective published in Environmental Science and Technology.

    Research plan

    Year 2024 was a very active research year at VOLT with many laboratory-based experiments and fieldwork campaigns. Research activities have progressed according to the original plan and no significant changes have been made. As our research tracks (RT) span across the different ecosystem/organism types and many studies contain elements from several RTs, we present the activities here based on the ecosystem/organism types.

    Within freshwater ecosystems, we participated in an intense fieldwork campaign with VOLT core collaborator Michael Steinke and Prof. Isabelle Laurion from INRA, Canada, at INRAE CARRTEL (The French National Research Institute for Agriculture, Food, and Environment) in France. VOLT PhD student Ellen Slater and Center Director Riikka Rinnan analyzed VOCs emitted by aquatic plants and plankton communities in Lake Geneva in several experiments (RT1). Water samples were also collected from four Alpine lakes to characterize how VOC concentrations and compositions vary in the water column and depend on the plankton community composition and environmental factors (RT2). Prof. Laurion and her PhD student Riley Hughes conducted a field expedition to Bylot Island in the Canadian Arctic to assess pond VOC concentrations in a dynamic permafrost landscape (RT1). Riley Hughes brought the samples to VOLT, learnt how to analyze the data, and this collaboration led to the planning of a second fieldwork campaign at the same location in 2025. VOLT PhD student Ida Roos Friis measured VOC emissions using floating chambers on three different Subarctic waterbodies in Abisko, Northern Sweden. The studies in High Arctic Canada and Subarctic Sweden aim to demonstrate how important the small water bodies with high organic matter content and high CO2 and CH4 emissions are as VOC sources to the atmosphere.

    Within marine ecosystems, VOLT post doc Mehrshad Foroughan in early 2024 prepared for a campaign onboard a German research vessel to measure sea water-atmosphere exchange of VOCs in collaboration with VOLT core collaborator Christa Marandino from GEOMAR, Germany. In late spring, just before the start, the campaign was cancelled due to the vessel’s engine failure. Discussions during a conference led to adoption of a “plan B”, and we shipped the instrumentation from Germany to Finland. Finally, sea water-atmosphere exchange measurements were successfully conducted on the Utö Atmospheric and Marine Research Station on the western shore of Utö Island in the Baltic Sea continuously July-October, 2024 (RT4). The preliminary data shows that marine VOC emissions consist of a high diversity of different VOCs and the ongoing work aims to connect the VOC data with information on environmental (e.g., temperature, salinity) and biotic (e.g., plankton biomass, chlorophyll) factors. VOLT PhD student Eve Galen has continued her studies of marine bacterial strains cultivated from the Baltic Sea. Her analyses reveal extensive emissions of VOCs and a striking overlap in emission of specific VOCs irrespective of the phylogenetic relatedness of the strains. Still, some VOCs are linked to specific taxa. These results are critical for understanding bacterial community dynamics-related VOC emissions in marine waters. Linked to these efforts, VOLT post doc Cecilia Costas Selas has in 2024 prepared and optimized methodology for a range of activities addressing VOC emissions from marine phytoplankton and the importance of bacteria – phytoplankton interactions.

    Our research into soils included both field and lab activities. The sabbatical stay at VOLT for Prof. Laura Meredith, University of Arizona, led to a collaboration and a field expedition to Svalbard. Prof. Meredith’s PhD student S. Marshall Ledford investigated VOC concentrations across a glacial forefield transect. He then brought soil samples to the laboratory at VOLT and conducted an experiment to distinguish production and consumption of VOCs (RT1). Active organisms and processes will be identified using metagenomics and metatranscriptomics. Another guest researcher, Dr. Arnaud Praplan, Finnish Meteorological Institute, brought with him soil samples from fertilization and tree species experiments in Finland to assess how VOC production rates link to microbial community compositions and other soil characteristics (RT1-2). Isolated soil bacterial cultures have been analyzed for their volatilomes under different salt concentrations in a study conducted by PhD student Miguel A. Salinas-García and post doc Kajsa Roslund. Complex soil microbial communities were also in the focus of an ambitious climate chamber experiment conducted by three VOLT PhD students in collaboration. The experiment used three different mini-ecosystems (i.e., mesocosms) collected in Subarctic Sweden for measuring ecosystem responses to heat waves and drought. Soil samples from this experiment were assessed for VOC production and uptake capacity by PhD student Neel Lindsby. Her results showed that drought conditions transformed the soil from a VOC sink to a source of many compounds (RT1-2). Ongoing metatranscriptomics analyses are expected to reveal what biochemical processes were up- and downregulated under drought.

    The same climate chamber experiment allowed study of plant thermal acclimation and plant responses to drought. PhD student Annika Engroff focused on responses in mosses and PhD student Marta Contreras-Serrano on responses in vascular plants (RT1-2). The results suggest that warmer climate and extreme weather events jointly impact Arctic plant phenology, photosynthesis, and the VOC emissions to the atmosphere, likely affecting the regional climate. Post doc Jolanta Rieksta measured VOC emissions of mountain birch populations in Northern Sweden, Norway, and Finland with the aim to assess chemical diversity of the species coupled to the tree age and genomics. In addition, Rieksta and post doc Amy Smart synthesized data from earlier field measurements to document how mountain birch VOC emissions change in response to insect outbreaks (RT3). PhD student Georgii Stoletov and post doc Nasmille Laerke-Mejia started method development to grow microorganisms that degrade the most abundant plant VOC, isoprene. They also started developing methods for using 13C-labelled isoprene to track microorganisms degrading isoprene on mosses (RT3). Postdoc Danillo Alvarenga carried out an experiment to quantify nitrogen (N) transfer from cyanobacteria to moss hosts (RT3). The samples are currently being analyzed at our collaborator’s nanoSIMS facilities in Lausanne, Switzerland. An extensive sampling of New Zealand’s mosses and liverworts has been completed to assess their potential contribution to N budgets via N fixation (RT2). New collaborations to extend our work on N fixation associated with tropical mosses and liverworts have been initiated with colleagues working in Brazil and Panama (RT2). Postdoc Yunyao Ma started to establish response curves of moss associated N fixation to temperature, light and moisture from different climates across the globe. These data on moss-associated N fixation will be incorporated into current vegetation models (RT4). A new collaboration was established with University of Lisbon and Globe Institute, University of Copenhagen, to assess chemical composition of the air inside caves in Southern Portugal and linking the air volatile composition with the microbiome and invertebrate data collected in the same caves.

PI Jing Tang collaborated with Dr. Twan van Noije from the Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute and has coupled the vegetation-emitted volatiles with atmospheric oxidation and aerosol-related processes.     Research plan

    VOLT’s research activities for the first 10 months of the center consist both of research efforts that have started prior to the official center period as well as new initiatives, which have commenced after the first research staff members were hired in August-September 2023. No changes have been made to the original research plan.

    The core activities started from method development and optimization to ensure an optimal starting point for the research activities. We purchased a new thermal desorber-gas chromatograph-mass spectrometer with a novel sample introduction method, called “Hisorb”, useful in immersive sampling. This method has been used for a few years within food sciences, and we have now conducted the – to our knowledge – first experiment optimizing the method for ecological studies. Our first three PhD students, each with their own focus area (soil, mosses, and marine plankton), collaborated with each other and VOLT’s lab manager in an experiment to modify applications for detection of volatiles in food products to use in soil, mosses and marine water samples. A publication that will directly feed to the aims of our research track 1 (RT1, separating volatile production and consumption) on this collaborative effort is in progress.

    Other activities within RT1 involve VOLT-affiliated scientists. Post doc Yi Jiao published a fundamental biogeochemistry paper (Jiao et al. 2023, Soil Biology & Biochemistry) on soil uptake of VOCs. This paper provides the first attempt to understand the reaction kinetics and temperature dependency of microbial VOC uptake in soil. Visiting post doc Wanying Zhang published a study addressing less studied soil emitted compound, carbonyl sulfide (OCS), production and consumption in Antarctic ecosystems with and without influence by sea animals, like penguins (Zhang et al. 2023, Communications Earth & Environment). Here, we highlighted the role of sea animal activities in shaping the soil-atmospheric exchange of OCS through interactions with soil chemical properties and microbial community. An affiliated PhD student Miguel A. Salinas-García conducted an experiment combining aspects from RT1 and RT2 (Effects of environmental factors). He isolated bacterial cultures from extreme Greenlandic soil environments, including permafrost and dry soil crust and assessed VOC production by these isolates under different salt additions. The three different studied isolates showed high VOC emission rates with intriguingly different emission blends; one characterized by sulfur compounds, another by carbon, and the third by nitrogen-based compounds.

    In an ongoing study, PhD student Eve Galen has been screening VOC emissions from a large number of bacterial cultures obtained from the Baltic Sea. The goal is to examine the volatilomes and address whether different bacteria emit different VOCs. This is an open and fundamental question in marine ecology, since marine VOC work is still in its infancy. We constructed a simple prototype for a floating chamber to assess VOC emissions from seasonal ponds in a rewetted peatland in Gribskov. MSc student Yinghuan Qin performed monthly measurements in different seasonal ponds identifying different volatilomes emitted from the ponds.

    Within RT2, we conducted climate chamber experiments to assess how environmental factors affect VOC emissions from Subarctic plants and soils. Visiting PhD Yue Gao from the College of Forestry, Sichuan Agricultural University compared CO2 response curves for dominant tundra shrubs grown under current natural and future higher CO2 concentrations. The results can be integrated with findings from a separate experiment, led by post doc Yi Jiao, in which mini-ecosystems composed of the same plant species and soil were also grown under two contrasting CO2 environments. The first results arising from this experiment are intriguing: high CO2 concentration causes increased monoterpene and sesquiterpene emissions, which is opposite from the conventional hypothesis based on earlier work that focused on a different VOC.

    Tana Wuyun and Lu Zhang from the Niinemets group at the Estonian University of Life Sciences and Zhejiang Agriculture and Forestry University respectively, conducted a laboratory experiment applying Selective Reagent Ionization-Mass Spectrometry (SRI-MS) on a Proton Transfer Reaction-Time of Flight-Mass Spectrometer (PTR-TOF-MS) to assess full volatilomes of different lichen species under contrasting temperatures and hydration levels. This experiment provides our first attempt in using the novel SRI-technology and serves us as a test case for data processing pipelines.

    In RT3, our work focuses on biotic interactions between different organisms and their influence on volatiles. MSc student Georgii Stoletov aimed at coupling oak tree isoprene emissions to isoprene degradation by microbial communities inhabiting the leaf surfaces. Weekly, he collected parallel data for emitted volatiles and leaf DNA throughout the full growing season from May to October. He developed methods for isoprene consumption assays together with the lab manager Kajsa Roslund, but instead of a simple assay the two encountered an exciting phenomenon, that will lead to follow-up studies: isoprene production, which should cease in minutes in dark conditions, continued for hours and even days in the detached oak leaves.

    Using top-of-the-line approaches (metatranscriptomics), we could show that mosses are less affected by their cyanobacterial colonizers than the cyanobacteria are by their moss hosts. Further, the moss-cyanobacteria symbioses is not a straightforward nutrient exchange mutualism, but something more complex and strongly dependent on environmental conditions (Alvarenga; Priemé & Rouks, 2024; Microbial Ecology).

    The activities in RT4 consisted of modelling activities ranging from accounting for different VOC sources to assessing atmospheric impacts. Visiting PhD student Zhanzhuo Chen parameterized leaf VOC emissions for a managed conifer forest, worked with adding litter VOC emissions into the model and assessed ecosystem-level VOC emissions against eddy-covariance observations. This study will provide us with a more complete model by considering VOC emissions from litter decomposition and forestry management. Post doc Daniel Thomas deployed a global atmospheric transport and chemistry model TM5 on our supercomputer system, Computerome, and fed a dynamic vegetation model LPJ-GUESS-simulated VOC emissions into TM5 to allow plant VOCs impact atmospheric composition. He set up a few sensitivity simulations and found the boreal and Arctic regions are very sensitive to plant emissions of VOCs. He was further investigating how long-range transported anthropogenic aerosols and VOCs from southern regions interact with plant and ocean VOC emissions in the high latitudes and how they together impact the Arctic climate. PI Tang extended the MEGAN emission model by accounting for herbivore- induced VOC emissions and further assessed atmospheric impacts using the fully coupled meteorology- chemistry-land model. She found that insect moth outbreaks can alter weather patterns by mediating birch VOC emissions from Fennoscandia.

    VOLT team conducted field work in different destinations. A team consisting of several scientists visited Abisko, Northern Sweden. Postdocs Amy Smart and Yi Jiao, PhD students Yue Gao and Simon Laursen and PI Rinnan continued long-term measurements of CO2 and volatile exchange in a Subarctic mountain birch forest ecosystem. An intense measurement campaign was conducted during an insect outbreak that happened in the site that had been measured also in the years up to the outbreak. This included insect surveys, quantification of leaf and stem volatiles of mountain birch as well as characterization of volatiles released from different ground vegetation types, and drone flights to couple spectral measurements of vegetation to the gas exchange data. Yi Jiao also conducted VOC measurements in two long-term field experiments: a soil warming experiment maintained by Lund University, Sweden, and a rain exclusion (i.e. drought) experiment maintained by University of Amsterdam, the Netherlands. At the end of the summer, PI Tang and VOLT PhD students Marta Contreras Serrano and Neel Lindsby traveled to Abisko area to collect mini-ecosystems (i.e., mesocosms) for measuring plant thermal acclimation and responses to heat wave and drought, and the experiments will be conducted in climate chambers in 2024. In August, Anders Priemé participated in an expedition to SE Greenland funded by the Swiss Leister Foundation to sample soils at this understudied part of Greenland, including sampling for an ongoing study of the root microbiome of Greenlandic willow, and finally measure soil fluxes of carbon dioxide and methane. In October, PI Tang visited three research stations at subtropical/tropical regions of China and discussed how to better integrate these stations’ observational data into models. She together with other LPJ-GUESS members organized 1 week summer school titled “Ecosystem modelling using LPJ-GUESS” at Beijing Normal University.

  • Recent funding for VOLT

    GRANTS: Recently, two VOLT members from the Ecosystem Ecology group, Kathrin Rousk and Mingye Yuan, have received grants for more studies on moss-cyanobacteria interactions.

    Kathrin has received DKK 2,384,988 from the Villum Foundation for a project titled “The symbiotic trinity – the next step in evolution? EvoMoss”, and Mingyue was awarded SEK 4,000,500 from Vetenskapsrådet (Swedish Research Council) for a project titled “How do moss-cyanobacteria associations drive nitrogen availability and community assembly during ecosystem succession?”. You can read a summary of each project below. Congratulations, Kathrin and Mingyue!

    The symbiotic trinity – the next step in evolution?

    Endosymbiosis, the process where one organism engulfs another, has played a key role in the evolution of life on Earth. One of the most significant examples is the origin of plants: a free-living cyanobacterium was engulfed by a host cell, eventually evolving into the chloroplast, the photosynthesizing organelle in plant cells. Last year, a groundbreaking discovery identified another potential endosymbiosis event: a free-living, nitrogen (N)-fixing cyanobacterium was engulfed by a marine alga, forming the marine nitroplast. Thisnew organelle supplies the host with newly fixed N, a key nutrient for all life. I propose here that we are at the dawn of a terrestrial nitroplost in moss-cyanobacteria symbioses, which can serve as a unique model system to unravel the evolution of an N-fixing organelle. Just as mitochondria and chloroplasts originated from ancient endosymbiotic events, the discovery of a terrestrial nitroplast would complete the “symbiotic trinity”, marking the third organelle acquired by plants.  

    How do moss-cyanobacteria associations drive nitrogen availability and community assembly during ecosystem succession?

    Primary succession occurs after severe disturbances that leave ecosystems devoid of most life. For life to establish, a supply of nutrients, in particular nitrogen (N), is required. Mosses are pioneers during primary succession, and all mosses host N-fixing cyanobacteria that can provide the needed N via biological nitrogen fixation. However, no study has yet resolved the dynamics of moss-cyanobacteria associations and their contribution to ecosystem N budget during primary succession, despite its importance for ecosystem development. I will fill this key knowledge gap in ecosystem ecology by 1) determining changes in moss-cyanobacteria associations in regard to species diversity and community composition of both partners as well as field cover along a successional gradient formed from glacier advances, 2) quantifying biological N fixation during primary successionin situ using isotope tracking; 3) elucidating the interactions between moss-cyanobacteria associations and vascular plants during primary succession using Grimes’ competitive-stress-ruderal (CSR) theory, uniquely applied to mosses. This project will uncover how ecosystems develop after severe disturbance and will elucidate the contribution of N fixation to ecosystem N cycling.

  • Decades-long international study reveals the complexity in Arctic ecosystem response to climate change

    A Rapidly Changing Arctic

    Despite Arctic ecosystems experiencing warming four times faster than the global average, little was known about the specific changes that occur in species diversity.

    A new study published in Nature by an international team of researchers lead by Dr. Mariana García Criado offers new insights into how Arctic plant communities are responding to rapid climate change.

    Decades of Data Across the Arctic

    The study, which used a compilation of over 42,000 plant species records and tracked more than 2,000 plant communities over four decades, found unexpected changes in species richness and composition. Rather than displaying a clear trend in biodiversity loss or gain, the study found that the number of plant species in most areas and on a larger-scale have remained relatively stable. Regardless, plant communities are experiencing significant shifts in species composition.

    Change Driven by Warming and Shrubification

    The study identified two key forces behind shifts in Arctic plant diversity: warming temperatures and shrubification. The spread of woody vegetation across the tundra leads to plant-plant competition, where plots with increasing shrub cover tend to lose species, while sites with more herbaceous plants see gains. This change can be attributed to the aboveground competition for light.

    No Signs of Homogenization

    Despite widespread changes, the researchers found no evidence that plant communities are becoming more similar across the Arctic. Instead, they are diverging, each following its own path depending on local conditions. This species reshuffling suggests important consequences for the wider Arctic food web, impacting ecosystem function, wildlife habitats, and the human communities that depend on tundra ecosystems.

    The Role of VOLT in the Study

    Over the years, VOLT researchers have accumulated valuable background datasets during the many volatile experiments and measurements across different locations. This information includes analyses of species composition, to give context for the detected volatile compounds. We have contributed all such relevant collected data to the large dataset behind this new article, with the combined dataset providing a base for a stronger study on large-scale plant diversity patterns. “The consistent collection and sharing of field-data allowed for new and unexpected findings, showing the value of long-term in situ ecological monitoring” says VOLT center director Riikka Rinnan.

  • Tremendous funding success for VOLT Center!

    Last week was filled with great news, as four(!) of our Center postdocs were awarded Marie Skłodowska-Curie Actions (MSCA) postdoctoral fellowships!

    Yunyao Ma
    Quantifying nitrogen fixation by moss-cyanobacteria associations from community to global scale by developing a novel process-based modelling approach
    I will develop a new process-based model incorporating nitrogen (N2) fixation in moss-cyanobacterial associations based on experimental observations from sites across the world and physiological mechanisms underlying moss-associated N2 fixation. Subsequently, I will apply this model to simulate the global pattern of N2 fixation and explore the mechanisms driving large variations in N2 fixation across different moss species.
    The Acronym is GoNiS (Global Moss-Cyanobacteria Nitrogen Fixation Simulation)

    Every year, the quality of the applications is excellent, the competition is fierce and the threshold is always very high. Yunyao scored a whopping 100% in her application!

    Wanying Zhang
    Reactive nitrogen and carbon Emissions From biocrusts at different Locations of the World
    This project aims to quantify the sources of reactive nitrogen and carbon emissions from biocrusts across various global locations. By integrating field measurements with mechanistic modeling, we seek to establish empirical relationships between emissions and environmental factors. The goal is to improve regional and global-scale predictions of these emissions and enhance our understanding of their atmospheric and climatic implications. 
    The acronym REFLOW was chosen to reflect the dynamic exchange of reactive nitrogen and carbon between biocrusts and the atmosphere. Coincidentally, it also carries the meaning of reprocessing and refining—something that resonates with both the scientific goals of this project and my own growth as a researcher.

    Mehrshad Foroughan
    Volatiles in Lake Ecosystem
    This project seeks to investigate VOCs dynamics in a lake ecosystem using advanced measurement technologies. The research outcomes will enhance our understanding of vital physical and biogeochemical processes in freshwater ecosystems, contribute to ecological theories, and introduce innovative aquatic monitoring practices based on VOCs variability.
    The acronym comes directly from the title: VOlatiles in LAKe Ecosystems (VOLAKE).

    Cecilia Costas Selas
    Role of biotic interactions in the production and composition of VOCs in marine waters
    The aim of this project is to study the biology behind VOC emissions in marine environments and in particular address the importance of biotic interactions: This includes the specific organisms producing VOCs, and under what circumstances, and how specific interactions influence VOC concentrations.
    For the first time, I will combine novel mass spectrometry-based methods for VOC analysis with cutting edge molecular methods to identify interactions, taxa and functionalities (genes) involved in marine VOC production.
    I will also address processes that affect plankton dynamics and the productivity of marine waters and associated VOC emissions that affect atmospheric composition and climate change.

  • 2024 VOLT Publications Highlights

    2024 was the first full year running for VOLT Center, and and there have been many exciting activities, collaborations, new people joining, cool instruments arriving and promising data being generated. This has also been reflected in our productivity in terms of scientific publications. You can find the full list here, and in this article we share some highlights selected by VOLT PIs:

    High temperature sensitivity of Arctic isoprene emissions explained by sedges

    (selected by Riikka Rinnan)

    This paper is a collaborative effort that sheds new light on why Arctic tundra ecosystems respond so strongly to rising temperatures in terms of their isoprene emissions. We show that sedges, grass-like plants that are abundant across the tundra and boreal peatlands, have higher temperature response than shrubs. Correct estimation of isoprene emissions is important in the warming Arctic as atmospheric isoprene has important implications on air quality and climate.

    You can read the article here.

    Air and Surface Temperatures Differently Drive Terrestrial Carbon and Water Cycles in the High Latitudes

    (selected by Jing Tang)

    High-latitude vegetation experiences different temperatures than the ambient air temperature.  Through driving one ecosystem model, LPJ-GUESS, with air and surface temperatures, this study revealed that (1) plant growths in the tundra are stimulated by surface temperature which is higher than air temperature in the summer months and (2) forcing temperature is crucial in determining high-latitude vegetation structure and productivity; and (3) Nor surface or air temperature can sufficiently capture plant temperature dynamics.

    You can read the paper here.

    The feather moss Hylocomium splendens affects the transcriptional profile of a symbiotic cyanobacterium in relation to acquisition and turnover of key nutrients.

    (selected by Kathrin Rousk)

    This is the first study reporting on the molecular interactions between mosses and associated, nitrogen (N) -fixing cyanobacteria using comparative transcriptomics. While the cyanobacterium increases nitrogen fixation rates when growing with the moss and key genes linked to nutrient turnover, the moss’ host does not change its transcriptomic profile when living together with the N-fixing cyanobacterium. Thus, the moss is less affected by the symbiotic interaction than cyanobacteria are, indicating a less tight symbioses than other cyanobacteria-plant interactions.

    You can find the full text here.

Scientific Annual Report 2024

August 28, 2026

Annual Highlights

The Center for Volatile Interactions (VOLT) aims to provide fundamental insights into the production, consumption, and transformations of volatile trace gasses in ecosystems. The year was active, productive and stimulating: VOLT hosted many guest researchers, established new collaborations, conducted several experiments and participated in successful field campaigns. All these activities reflect VOLT’s spirit: aiming for collaboration instead of competition.

VOLT’s target organisms and systems include plant-soil continuums, cryptogams (mosses, lichens, and organic crusts), oceans, lakes, and the microbial communities living in these systems. In 2024, our work included elements from each of these, but had some emphasis on the aquatic systems. We kicked off the year with a mini-symposium on freshwater trace gas fluxes to discuss challenges and possibilities facing our work. The mini-symposium had about 20 participants, including international visitors from three countries, colleagues from the rest of the department as well as VOLT members.

In 2024, VOLT developed new methods and optimized old ones for quantification of volatilomes (mixtures of different volatile organic compounds, VOCs) in water. The methods include immersive sampling of VOCs on probes, purging and trapping of volatiles out of water, operation of floating chambers to measure VOC emissions from ponds and lakes to the atmosphere as well as direct measurement of sea atmosphere exchange of VOCs using micrometeorological methods. These methodological advances facilitate efficient characterization of VOC emissions from aquatic ecosystems.

In addition to the internal center events, such as the annual retreat and biweekly VOLT-all meetings and seminars, the center members were active in organizing international conferences. For example, VOLT PIs Lasse Riemann and Jing Tang chaired the Gordon Research Conference on Marine Microbes and the Gordon Research Seminar on Biogenic Hydrocarbons and the Atmosphere, respectively. The center received recognition in the form of funding: A new Marie Curie post doc fellow, Dr. Nasmille Laerke-Mejia, was welcomed to VOLT with a project aiming to quantify microbial degradation of isoprene, an important VOC, in Arctic ecosystems. Two center PIs, Kathrin Rousk and Jing Tang, received project grants from the Independent Research Fund Denmark. VOLT PI Anders Priemé was selected as the Teacher of the Year at the Department of Biology and nominated for Faculty’s teaching prize.

In terms of publications, in 2024 we want to highlight our work presenting perspectives in the research landscape:

Organization

The VOLT Steering Committee (SC), consisting of center director Riikka Rinnan and co-PIs Lasse Riemann, Anders Priemé, Kathrin Rousk, and Jing Tang, convened twice a month to ensure common direction and commitment of personnel towards shared goals. The Center administrator also attended these meetings.

VOLT staff meets casually in the common office corridor and the lunchroom. In addition, several meetings are arranged to allow efficient transfer of knowledge: VOLT all meeting with scientific seminar talks every two weeks, VOC lunch meeting every two weeks, group meetings organized by PIs, a journal club organized by the early career researchers, and many social gatherings outside office hours.

The Scientific Advisory Board (SAB) for VOLT comprises Jaana Bäck, Jörg Peter Schnitzler, Hans-Peter Grossart, and Colin Murrell, all leading experts with complementary backgrounds relevant to VOLT. In 2024, the SAB remotely attended parts of the program for the Annual Visit by the DNRF. This was followed by discussions about progress and future strategy between SAB and the VOLT PIs.

VOLT laboratory manager, Kajsa Roslund, started in her own post doc project funded by a Finnish foundation in May 2024. We initiated recruitment for the lab manager position well in advance to secure some overlap, but did not find the right person for this highly specialized position. We found a solution with Kajsa continuing with 25% as lab manager in May-December 2024. In addition, we recruited an instrument specialist, Thomas Holst, with 40% contribution of full working time in the period July 22-December 2024. We finally found a new lab manager via search committee work and personal contacts: Suman Som started in this position in November 2024. Another addition to our TAP staff was hiring a student assistant with focus on communication, Sofie Kalum Schou.

VOLT recruited three post docs (PDs). Mehrshad Foroughan and Cecilia Costas Selas started in PD1 and PD2 positions, working with marine VOC fluxes in research tracks 4 and 3, respectively. Tamás Plaszkó was recruited to the data science focused position (approved by DNRF in 2023), in a role where he contributes to other projects with data science expertise as well as leads the database and synthesis efforts. Two new VOLT PhD students started in 2024, Ellen Salamon Slater as PhD4 (focus on freshwater) and Georgii Stoletov as PhD6 (focus on leaf microbial communities). In addition, four post docs and one PhD student joined VOLT with funding from other grants.

Recruitment and gender strategy

VOLT aims to attract the best candidates internationally by clearly communicating the unique research and training offered in the center. Recruitment is done using public advertisement of positions both via official channels, personal contacts, and social media. Usually hiring interviews are held first online and then on site and the selection is based on excellence. VOLT endorses diversity, as is demonstrated by the gender-balanced and multinational team of PIs and collaborators. The PI team comprises three females and two males, and collaborators are roughly 50% of each gender. Among post doc, PhD student and TAP staff, 53% are female and 47% male. VOLT members represent 17 different nationalities. The inclusive, open, and collaborative research environment is built to allow different approaches and personalities to contribute to a unique, creative space for science, which will translate into innovation and high productivity.

Research integrity and data management

VOLT is committed to the Danish Code of Conduct for Research Integrity and to following FAIR principles for scientific management of data. A working group formed of post doc/data scientist Tamás Plaszkó, Jing Tang, and Anders Priemé investigated needs and possibilities, taking into account the complexity and multidimensionality of the research data from VOLT, and planned the architecture and set up for a long- term database for the Center. The database was established in the Electronic Research Data Archive at University of Copenhagen (ERDA). Instructions, meta-data and project description templates were created in 2024 with the aim to launch the database in January 2025.

In addition, the raw data generated are stored in several locations to avoid accidental data loss, such as personal network drives, with safety copies at local drives and shared network drives. The data safety framework of ERDA has further strengthened our efforts to secure our data now and in the future. Shared working folders on ERDA are used for collaborative work, internally and externally.

When starting at VOLT, all employees and students are introduced to and sign a document committing to responsible conduct of research, which is stored by the Center Administrator. All PhD students and postdocs also attend a mandatory course on responsible research conduct offered by University of Copenhagen. Protocols, infrastructure booking schedules, etc. are shared and stored online on VOLT’s common network drive provided by the University of Copenhagen. In 2024, VOLT received bronze accreditation in the Laboratory Efficiency Assessment Framework (LEAF) aiming to more sustainable and efficient lab practices.

Research plan

Year 2024 was a very active research year at VOLT with many laboratory-based experiments and fieldwork campaigns. Research activities have progressed according to the original plan and no significant changes have been made. As our research tracks (RT) span across the different ecosystem/organism types and many studies contain elements from several RTs, we present the activities here based on the ecosystem/organism types.

Within freshwater ecosystems, we participated in an intense fieldwork campaign with VOLT core collaborator Michael Steinke and Prof. Isabelle Laurion from INRA, Canada, at INRAE CARRTEL (The French National Research Institute for Agriculture, Food, and Environment) in France. VOLT PhD student Ellen Slater and Center Director Riikka Rinnan analyzed VOCs emitted by aquatic plants and plankton communities in Lake Geneva in several experiments (RT1). Water samples were also collected from four Alpine lakes to characterize how VOC concentrations and compositions vary in the water column and depend on the plankton community composition and environmental factors (RT2). Prof. Laurion and her PhD student Riley Hughes conducted a field expedition to Bylot Island in the Canadian Arctic to assess pond VOC concentrations in a dynamic permafrost landscape (RT1). Riley Hughes brought the samples to VOLT, learnt how to analyze the data, and this collaboration led to the planning of a second fieldwork campaign at the same location in 2025. VOLT PhD student Ida Roos Friis measured VOC emissions using floating chambers on three different Subarctic waterbodies in Abisko, Northern Sweden. The studies in High Arctic Canada and Subarctic Sweden aim to demonstrate how important the small water bodies with high organic matter content and high CO2 and CH4 emissions are as VOC sources to the atmosphere.

Within marine ecosystems, VOLT post doc Mehrshad Foroughan in early 2024 prepared for a campaign onboard a German research vessel to measure sea water-atmosphere exchange of VOCs in collaboration with VOLT core collaborator Christa Marandino from GEOMAR, Germany. In late spring, just before the start, the campaign was cancelled due to the vessel’s engine failure. Discussions during a conference led to adoption of a “plan B”, and we shipped the instrumentation from Germany to Finland. Finally, sea water-atmosphere exchange measurements were successfully conducted on the Utö Atmospheric and Marine Research Station on the western shore of Utö Island in the Baltic Sea continuously July-October, 2024 (RT4). The preliminary data shows that marine VOC emissions consist of a high diversity of different VOCs and the ongoing work aims to connect the VOC data with information on environmental (e.g., temperature, salinity) and biotic (e.g., plankton biomass, chlorophyll) factors. VOLT PhD student Eve Galen has continued her studies of marine bacterial strains cultivated from the Baltic Sea. Her analyses reveal extensive emissions of VOCs and a striking overlap in emission of specific VOCs irrespective of the phylogenetic relatedness of the strains. Still, some VOCs are linked to specific taxa. These results are critical for understanding bacterial community dynamics-related VOC emissions in marine waters. Linked to these efforts, VOLT post doc Cecilia Costas Selas has in 2024 prepared and optimized methodology for a range of activities addressing VOC emissions from marine phytoplankton and the importance of bacteria – phytoplankton interactions.

Our research into soils included both field and lab activities. The sabbatical stay at VOLT for Prof. Laura Meredith, University of Arizona, led to a collaboration and a field expedition to Svalbard. Prof. Meredith’s PhD student S. Marshall Ledford investigated VOC concentrations across a glacial forefield transect. He then brought soil samples to the laboratory at VOLT and conducted an experiment to distinguish production and consumption of VOCs (RT1). Active organisms and processes will be identified using metagenomics and metatranscriptomics. Another guest researcher, Dr. Arnaud Praplan, Finnish Meteorological Institute, brought with him soil samples from fertilization and tree species experiments in Finland to assess how VOC production rates link to microbial community compositions and other soil characteristics (RT1-2). Isolated soil bacterial cultures have been analyzed for their volatilomes under different salt concentrations in a study conducted by PhD student Miguel A. Salinas-García and post doc Kajsa Roslund. Complex soil microbial communities were also in the focus of an ambitious climate chamber experiment conducted by three VOLT PhD students in collaboration. The experiment used three different mini-ecosystems (i.e., mesocosms) collected in Subarctic Sweden for measuring ecosystem responses to heat waves and drought. Soil samples from this experiment were assessed for VOC production and uptake capacity by PhD student Neel Lindsby. Her results showed that drought conditions transformed the soil from a VOC sink to a source of many compounds (RT1-2). Ongoing metatranscriptomics analyses are expected to reveal what biochemical processes were up- and downregulated under drought.

The same climate chamber experiment allowed study of plant thermal acclimation and plant responses to drought. PhD student Annika Engroff focused on responses in mosses and PhD student Marta Contreras-Serrano on responses in vascular plants (RT1-2). The results suggest that warmer climate and extreme weather events jointly impact Arctic plant phenology, photosynthesis, and the VOC emissions to the atmosphere, likely affecting the regional climate. Post doc Jolanta Rieksta measured VOC emissions of mountain birch populations in Northern Sweden, Norway, and Finland with the aim to assess chemical diversity of the species coupled to the tree age and genomics. In addition, Rieksta and post doc Amy Smart synthesized data from earlier field measurements to document how mountain birch VOC emissions change in response to insect outbreaks (RT3). PhD student Georgii Stoletov and post doc Nasmille Laerke-Mejia started method development to grow microorganisms that degrade the most abundant plant VOC, isoprene. They also started developing methods for using 13C-labelled isoprene to track microorganisms degrading isoprene on mosses (RT3). Postdoc Danillo Alvarenga carried out an experiment to quantify nitrogen (N) transfer from cyanobacteria to moss hosts (RT3). The samples are currently being analyzed at our collaborator’s nanoSIMS facilities in Lausanne, Switzerland. An extensive sampling of New Zealand’s mosses and liverworts has been completed to assess their potential contribution to N budgets via N fixation (RT2). New collaborations to extend our work on N fixation associated with tropical mosses and liverworts have been initiated with colleagues working in Brazil and Panama (RT2). Postdoc Yunyao Ma started to establish response curves of moss associated N fixation to temperature, light and moisture from different climates across the globe. These data on moss-associated N fixation will be incorporated into current vegetation models (RT4). A new collaboration was established with University of Lisbon and Globe Institute, University of Copenhagen, to assess chemical composition of the air inside caves in Southern Portugal and linking the air volatile composition with the microbiome and invertebrate data collected in the same caves.

Modelling has been applied in and integrated with several field- and laboratory-based sub- projects linking RT4 with RTs 1-3. PI Jing Tang collaborated with Dr. Twan van Noije from the Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute and has coupled the vegetation-emitted volatiles with atmospheric oxidation and aerosol-related processes. The coupled model is still under testing and can deliver quantitative assessments of the climatic and atmospheric impacts of VOC emissions linked to vegetation dynamics and plant physiology. Postdoc Hongliang Ma started to develop a machine-learning model to produce the first global canopy temperature product by accounting for land, vegetation and canopy information and surface energy balance. The delivered canopy temperature products can be used to better understand the temperature-associated plant processes at global scale. PhD student Ida Roos Friis started to do biogeochemical model simulations for individual Arctic lakes with a focus on greenhouse gas emissions. She is working on comparing the modelled CO2 and CH4 emissions with the observation data she collected in summer 2024.
